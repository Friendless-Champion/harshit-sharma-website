3D-Printing Policy Research.

Published Researcher · SSRN

I analyze five state ghost gun bills and argue that broad definitions could threaten legitimate 3D-printing uses in schools, engineering, and small businesses.

What the work
looked like.

Lawmakers are writing ghost gun laws as if 3D printers were desktop toys. In this paper, I analyze five 2025 and 2026 bills from Washington, New York, California, Colorado, and Illinois. I argue that broad definitions could reach CNC machines and other devices that make objects from digital models, while one bill’s required “blocking technology” is not technically available: G-code translates a model into movement instructions rather than identifying what a part is.

Ghost guns pose a real public-safety concern. My argument is that some of these bills go further than that goal requires and could burden educators, small businesses, and student engineers, including my own FRC team. I interviewed three people for the paper, including a teammate who described prototyping a gear in PLA before moving to nylon.

I wrote this 10-page paper, including its title page and references, in May 2026 for ENG 1108: Writing and Research Skills at Inver Hills Community College. I conducted the research and wrote the full article under my professor’s guidance and feedback, then submitted it to SSRN independently. SSRN posted it on June 18, 2026.
